## 3. Five conclusions

### 1. The two splice directions differ **asymmetrically in difficulty**, and by a wide margin

- **Cat head + eagle body (easy)**: worked at the first try. The cat head is a "small, generic, round" part, and placing it on an eagle body does not fight the eagle's body plan.
- **Eagle head + cat body (hard)**: degenerates straight into an eagle. The eagle's **body plan is too strong**—the word "eagle" drags wings, talons and feathers along with it,
  while the cat's body plan is too generic (quadruped + fur) to hold it down.

> **Lesson**: animal splicing is not a symmetric operation. The **body** of a strong species (eagle / lion / antlers) is very hard to overwrite;
> to make a strong species serve as the "head", extra work is required (see conclusion 3).

### 2. `with no wings at all` **does not work** — positive exclusions are unreliable

Both the A and C versions grew wings. Z-Image-Turbo's workflow uses `ConditioningZeroOut` and **has no negative prompt**,
so "excluding a feature" can only be written positively (`with no wings at all`), and the model basically ignores it.

> **Lesson**: exclusion-style constraints must go **negative**. This is exactly why this project switches to Qwen-Image in the final round
> (it has true negatives), and it is another confirmation of the bone-china-doll R34 finding that "excluding a feature must be written in the negative".

### 3. B (visible seam) is the **only semantics that actually makes the "hard direction" splice**—but the reason is not the stitching

This is the most valuable finding of the round. The sentence in B:

```
...the whole animal looks assembled from two different animals like a museum specimen.
```

gave the model **permission to "mix two species"**. Whereas the phrasing in A/C is:

```
a single animal whose head is entirely a golden eagle's and whose body is entirely a domestic cat's
```

This was read as **"draw one self-consistent animal"** → so the model picked the stronger side (the eagle) and finished it.

> **Inference**: R1 mixed two things into one variable—
> **(a) mixing permission** (assembled from two animals) and **(b) a visible seam** (stitching).
> What actually worked was (a); the stitching was only a side effect.
> **R2's primary hypothesis**: taking (a) out on its own and removing (b) should yield an animal that is **both spliced together and free of Frankenstein stitching**.

### 4. C (surreal) is a **no-op** and should be deleted

In both subject pairs, C and A are almost indistinguishable to the eye; `pngdiff`'s `mean_abs_diff` points the same way (comparisons within the same subject):

| Comparison within the same subject | mean_abs_diff |
|---|---|
| C vs A (owl / eagle-headed cat) | **8.9 / 9.5** ← the smallest of all comparisons |
| A vs B (owl / eagle-headed cat) | 18.3 / 25.7 |

> **Lesson**: do not write adjective-style semantic sentences ("slightly wrong / uncanny / impossible proportions") just because they "sound more artistic".
> **Abstract adjectives do not change the image**, which is the same class of phenomenon as bone-china-doll's "naming an object is reliable, facial micro-makeup is not".
> Either give a **concrete drawable noun**, or the variable should be deleted.

### 5. The same seed lets the spliced body **reuse the control's composition**—a free bonus that should be hardened into discipline

With all 8 images on the same seed, `owl-seamless`'s camera / pose / background **almost coincide** with the `eagle` control,
and "the same eagle with a cat head" is readable at a glance. This greatly improves the legibility of A/B/C and of the "head-body mismatch".

> **Discipline**: in the control round of a matrix-type project, **all images share the seed**; do not give each image a different seed.

## 4. R2 plan (**already overturned by R2–R5, see [r02.md](r02.en.md)**)

> ⚠️ Item 1 below (deleting the wings clause) and the "mixing permission" hypothesis that item 5 relies on
> have both been disproved by R2's **four single-variable controls**: deleting the wings clause changed the image not at all;
> mixing permission had no visible effect in any of the four tests. What actually worked was **word order**.
> This section keeps the original text to document R1's misattribution; the conclusions in [r02.md](r02.en.md) take precedence.

| # | Change | Basis |
|---|------|------|
| 1 | **Separate "mixing permission" from "visible seam"**: add semantics D = `assembled from two different animals` (kept) + **delete** `stitching` (remove the seam) | Conclusion 3 |
| 2 | **Delete C (surreal)**, move its budget to D | Conclusion 4 |
| 3 | The eagle-headed cat's head **must exclude cat ears**: write it explicitly as a bird's head (`a bird's head with no external ears`), otherwise it drifts into "beak + cat ears" | R1 version B measured |
| 4 | For the eagle-headed cat, **name the cat's four parts**: `four feline legs`, `a long ringed tabby tail`, `paw pads`, `sheathed claws`—R1 proves that naming them gets them drawn (version B produced cat legs and a tabby tail) | R1 version B measured |
| 5 | Switch to **Qwen-Image 2512** (6–13 min/image, 3 images/round) and use true negatives to suppress `wings / feathers on body` | Conclusion 2 |

## 5. Consolidation (reusable across projects)

46. **Animal/species splicing is asymmetric by direction**: a strong species serving as the "body" is very hard to overwrite; as the "head" it is the opposite.
47. **Positive exclusions are unreliable; exclusion constraints must go negative** (another confirmation of bone-china-doll lesson 46).
48. **"Combination/assembly" wording is the permission that makes the model truly mix species**; writing "one self-consistent animal" is read as "pick one side and finish it".
49. **Abstract adjective-style semantic sentences ≈ a no-op**; `pngdiff`'s `mean_abs_diff` can serve as a quantitative gauge of "how much this sentence actually did".
50. **A matrix-type project shares one seed across all images**, letting the spliced body reuse the control's composition and greatly improving control legibility.
